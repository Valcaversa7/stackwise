## What happened on the morning of 26 August

On the morning of Wednesday 26 August, a torrent of mud, water, rock and ice surged out of the high valleys on the Nepal–China border and swept through Rasuwa district, in the Himalayas of north-central Nepal. Within hours it had killed at least 160 people, buried riverside settlements, snapped dozens of bridges and left more than 1,300 people missing on both sides of the border.

The disaster struck near Gyirong, the border crossing between Nepal and China's Tibet autonomous region, and followed the Bhote Koshi river downstream. The flood also hit the Chinese side of the border, where a mudslide devastated the Gyirong port of entry. Nepal's Prime Minister Balendra Shah's office put the damage at at least 19 vehicle bridges and around 40 kilometres of roads swept away, with schools and hydropower projects among what was lost.

Because the worst-affected valleys are cut off by the very roads and bridges the flood destroyed, rescue work has had to proceed by helicopter, on foot, and by drone reconnaissance — and the casualty numbers are still being verified. Treat every figure in this article as "as of the time of writing": the tolls were still rising throughout 27 August.

## How a glacier collapse became a flood

Scientists say the flood was most likely a glacial outburst. A large section of glacier — one report puts the broken-off ice at roughly half a mile long — calved away and collapsed in a single avalanche of ice, rock and meltwater. That debris dammed and then failed almost instantly, sending a wall of slurry down the valley at speeds that gave riverside residents no time to react.

The International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development, based in Kathmandu, said a glacier avalanche is the most likely trigger. The mechanics matter, because this is not a "one-in-a-century rainstorm" event: the same failure mode — a glacier or glacial lake giving way as mountain temperatures rise — has hit this exact region before.

The region also carries the ordinary monsoon load on top. Thousands of people live in settlements directly on the banks of the Bhote Koshi and the Trishuli downstream, and the flood's slurry carried boulders, trees and whole building foundations with it. Where it stopped, it deposited metres of silt that will take years to clear.

## The death toll and the missing

The numbers, as reported by Nepal's police, Chinese state media and the Nepali tourism board, with the caveat that verification is still ongoing:

- **Nepal:** more than 160 confirmed dead, with 41 people hospitalised after the flood. Police reported 162 bodies recovered late on Wednesday; officials subsequently put the toll at 165.
- **China (Tibet):** three confirmed deaths in Gyirong County, with the number of missing there reported at 558, of whom 260 were foreign nationals.
- **Missing overall:** more than 1,300 across the border area as of 27 August. Nepal reported around 400 missing people.
- **Indian nationals:** 133 Indians are missing, including 37 from Tamil Nadu — many of them part of a pilgrimage group travelling to Mount Kailash, the sacred Hindu peak in Tibet.
- **Other foreign nationals:** roughly 340 foreign tourists are accounted for as missing, with the Nepal Tourism Board's breakdown putting the United States at 47, Australia at 34, Britain at 33 and Canada at 24.

The United States State Department said it is "closely tracking" the situation for American citizens. Canada's Prime Minister Mark Carney called the floods "absolutely devastating", and Australia's Prime Minister Anthony Albanese confirmed 34 Australians are missing. The UN Secretary-General said UN teams and partners were mobilising supplies and personnel in Nepal.

The missing counts include people who were travelling, trekking, or on pilgrimage through one of the most popular mountain corridors in the Himalayas at the height of the summer season.

## What the flood destroyed

The damage list read like an infrastructure audit of the entire district:

1. **Bridges.** At least 19 vehicle bridges swept away. Every crossing on the affected river sections is gone, which is why relief cannot simply be driven in.
2. **Roads.** Around 40 kilometres of road destroyed. Drone checks on the Chinese side found all roads leading to the Gyirong border crossing "completely destroyed".
3. **Settlements.** Riverside villages downstream from the outburst were buried under metres of silt. In the towns of Syapru Besi and Timure in Rasuwa, residents described settlements "completely swept away" — with relatives still missing.
4. **Power and services.** Hydropower projects washed out; communications lines cut. Rescue helicopters could not land in the hardest-hit valleys while the river ran above its normal course.
5. **Gyirong port.** The border crossing itself was overwhelmed on the Chinese side; security footage verified by multiple news agencies shows crowds fleeing a road before the torrent arrived.

Local officials described the scene in words that are doing the rounds in Kathmandu: "There is devastation everywhere we look."

## India's response: airlifts, the embassy, 133 missing Indians

India's response began within hours of the first reports and has escalated over 26–27 August:

**The phone call.** Prime Minister Narendra Modi spoke to his Nepali counterpart Balendra Shah on Wednesday and conveyed condolences for the lives lost. "The people of India stand in solidarity with their sisters and brothers in Nepal at this difficult time," Modi said. "India is ready to provide all possible humanitarian assistance to Nepal. Our teams are coordinating closely on rescue and relief efforts." President Droupadi Murmu also posted that India stands ready to support Nepal's relief and rescue efforts.

**The first airlift.** On Wednesday night, an Indian Air Force C-130J took off from Hindon airbase near Delhi at around 8:35 pm for Kathmandu carrying **10 tonnes of humanitarian assistance and disaster relief (HADR) supplies plus essential medicines** — External Affairs Minister Dr. S. Jaishankar's exact words from his announcement. The loadout, as reported, included temporary shelters, blankets, hygiene kits, solar lamps and medicines.

**More flying.** A second IAF transport — a C-17 — was expected to depart for Nepal on Thursday, and the IAF's wider transport fleet (C-17, C-130J and C-295 aircraft) has been kept on standby for further relief operations. The NDRF (National Disaster Response Force) is also on standby.

**The embassy and the missing Indians.** The Embassy of India in Kathmandu is coordinating closely with the Government of Nepal on the early rescue and relief of Indian nationals, per the Ministry of External Affairs. With 133 Indians missing — many on the Kailash pilgrimage route — the embassy has become the focal point for families in India.

**Nepal's thanks.** Nepal's Foreign Minister Shisir Khanal publicly thanked India and the external affairs minister for the relief materials.

The broader international response is also arriving: the World Bank has pledged 25 billion Nepali rupees in assistance, and the UN is mobilising teams and supplies. India's airlifts are the most visible element of it so far.

## The bigger picture: a valley that floods on repeat

This is not the first time this valley has failed. In **August 2025**, the same corridor experienced a similar flash flood after a glacial lake in neighbouring Tibet overflowed due to high mountain temperatures; nine people were killed and the critical bridge linking Nepal and China was damaged. The 2026 event is, in the language of mountain-disaster scientists, a recurrence of the same hazard class: a warming glacier system shedding mass and melt faster than the valleys below can absorb.

That recurrence is the uncomfortable part of the story. Rasuwa and its border towns have been developing quickly — treks, pilgrimages, hydropower, trade through Gyirong — while sitting downstream of exactly the kind of unstable ice that climate change is accelerating. The flood did not just kill people; it removed the infrastructure the region was just starting to rely on, in the same place it removed it a year ago.

## Where to help

If you want to help, the channels that actually work in a disaster like this:

- **Official relief channels.** The Government of Nepal is coordinating through its National Disaster Risk Management Authority; the **Nepal Red Cross Society** is the designated national humanitarian partner receiving international donations for this response.
- **For Indian citizens and families.** The Embassy of India in Kathmandu and the MEA are the single point of coordination for the missing Indian nationals — call or write to the embassy rather than approaching field teams directly.
- **Money beats supplies.** Airlift capacity is the bottleneck, not blankets. Cash donations to the channels above convert directly into the transport that is actually missing.
- **Be suspicious of ad-hoc appeals** circulating on social media. Verify the organisation before you send money — disaster weeks are when scammers work hardest.
