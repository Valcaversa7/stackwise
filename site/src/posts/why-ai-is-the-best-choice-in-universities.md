## What the choice actually is

When people say "studying AI at university", they usually mean one of three different things, and the advice differs for each.

**A major or concentration in AI, machine learning or computer science with an AI focus.** Four years of the full pipeline: the mathematics, the systems, the evaluation, and enough breadth to know when a problem is not an AI problem at all.

**AI as a core skill on top of another degree.** A biology student who can train and audit models, a law student who can build defensible document analysis, an engineer who can ship a working inference pipeline. This is where most of the actual hiring is happening, because the interesting work sits at the seam between a domain and the models.

**AI as a literacy.** Knowing how the tools work well enough to use them well and check them honestly. Every graduate will need this, and it costs a single good course, not a degree.

All three are defensible. The argument in this article is for the first two: that right now, more than at any point in the last twenty years, the expected return on spending your student years acquiring deep AI skills is the best of any option on the menu. Not because the field is guaranteed to stay hot, but because the gap between what the economy needs and what the pipeline produces is at its widest.

## The labour market argument

Hype cycles produce buzzwords. This one produced a cost structure. Training and serving models is expensive, which means the companies that pay for it are companies with real revenue, not speculators with a pitch deck. That distinction is the single most important difference between this cycle and the ones that came before, and it is why the demand is durable even if the marketing is not.

What employers are actually asking for falls into three buckets:

1. **People who can deploy.** The bottleneck in most organisations is not model access — it is getting a model reliably into a production system: data pipelines, evaluation harnesses, cost control, monitoring for drift, and the unglamorous failure modes. This is engineering work, and it is where the job density is highest.
2. **People who can evaluate.** Most AI projects die from bad evaluation, not bad models. Someone who can design a test that actually measures whether the system got better — rather than whether it looks better in a demo — is rarer than a good model.
3. **People who can keep the maths.** Research roles and the frontier of applied work still require a working grasp of probability, optimisation and linear algebra at a level that cannot be bolted on later.

The supply side of that equation is the uncomfortable part of the case. Bootcamps, courses and "AI engineer" certifications have multiplied, and the flood of certificates with no depth behind them is already visible in hiring pipelines. The market is not short of people who can call an API. It is short of people who understand what the model is doing well enough to fix it when it fails. That is exactly the distinction university training makes, and the premium is paid there.

## Why university, not a bootcamp

The honest version of the argument: a university degree is not the fastest path to the first job. It is the best path to the career. Several things make the difference.

**The mathematics is not optional and cannot be compressed.** Linear algebra, probability, calculus and optimisation are not decoration at the start of an AI curriculum. They are the language the field speaks a year in, when the frameworks you memorised stop covering what you need. Bootcamps skip this layer because it is slow and it does not produce a demo. People who skipped it hit a wall at exactly the moment they are supposed to start being senior.

**Research access is a resource, not a perk.** The best university programmes sit next to labs with compute, datasets and people publishing. A student who spends two years reading papers critically, reproducing results and shipping a small genuine contribution comes out with something no bootcamp can issue: a track record of independent technical judgement.

**The degree is a filter you use once.** Banks, insurers, healthcare, government and defence hire AI people in volume, and most of them still run their hiring through accredited degrees because the alternative is to audit every certificate. If your goal includes any of those sectors, the filter matters.

**Four years is a feature: it spans a hype cycle.** The tools you learn in year one will have been refactored by year three. The people who finish strong are the ones whose value sits in fundamentals and judgement, which is precisely what a structured programme is built to install.

## The leverage argument

There is a second, quieter case that has nothing to do with whether "AI" itself stays a large industry.

AI is a multiplier. A researcher who can run models gets ten times the experiments per semester. A law student who can evaluate an analysis pipeline gets a skill no pre-2023 graduate had. An engineer who can specify and audit an agent system is more valuable in every team, not just AI teams.

That means the best expected value of studying AI is not even a bet on the AI industry winning. It is a bet on the fact that people who can work with these systems well will be leveraged in every industry that adopts them, for the rest of their careers. The skill decays slower than any specific model, because the underlying work — data, evaluation, systems, cost, risk — is the same work software has always needed, only with a new component.

## What the hype-cycle objections get wrong

The standard objections are worth taking seriously, and most of them are wrong for this cycle specifically.

**"Every new tech becomes oversaturated."** Usually true, for skills that were easy to learn. The dot-com era's "web developer" premium collapsed because the skill bar was low and the supply elastic. The skills that carry a premium right now — evaluation design, systems reliability, mathematical depth — are hard to acquire quickly, which is what keeps the supply in check.

**"The frameworks will all be obsolete."** Correct, and irrelevant, if your value is in the fundamentals and the judgement. Frameworks are the easy twenty percent. The premium is on the other eighty percent, which is stable.

**"AI will do the work."** If that were true, the companies buying the work would not be hiring the people. The cost structure we mentioned earlier — compute, data, evaluation — is why the bottleneck is human judgement, not model access.

## How to make the four years count

The argument only holds if the student does the work that separates depth from decoration. Five things that actually move the needle:

1. **Do the maths deliberately.** If the linear algebra and probability courses feel like overhead, that is the moment to push, not to skim. Everything downstream gets easier or impossible based on this.
2. **Ship systems, not notebooks.** The strongest portfolios are small deployed things with real data, real evaluation and real failure modes written down honestly. A boring system that survives contact with reality beats a flashy demo that cannot.
3. **Read papers until it stops being fun, then read some more.** The ability to read a paper, identify the actual contribution and find the weakness is a skill with a direct price tag in hiring.
4. **Learn to evaluate before you learn to impress.** Build a reflex: any time a model output looks good, ask what test would catch it being wrong. That reflex is the rarest and the most valuable thing in the room.
5. **Get into a lab or an internship early.** Independent judgement is installed by doing real work with senior people, not by accumulating coursework.

The window is open right now: the demand is real, the infrastructure is being built, and the depth gap between people with and without serious training has never been wider. Hype does not pay salaries. The cost structure does — and it is widening the gap for people who did the hard four years, not the easy certification.
