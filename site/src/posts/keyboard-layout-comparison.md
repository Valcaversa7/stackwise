## The experiment

Ninety days, three layouts, thirty days each, in the order QWERTY → Colemak-DH → Dvorak. Same keyboard, same chair, same working hours. Every day I typed a fixed 500-word corpus plus whatever work I was actually doing, and logged words per minute, error rate and a subjective discomfort score.

The learning curves were real and brutal. Colemak-DH cost me roughly eleven days below my QWERTY baseline; Dvorak took eighteen and never fully recovered within the month.

## The results

| Layout | Day-30 speed | Error rate | Discomfort (0–10) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| QWERTY | 92 wpm | 2.1% | 5 |
| Colemak-DH | 88 wpm | 1.6% | 3 |
| Dvorak | 71 wpm | 2.8% | 3 |

Colemak-DH ended the month within 4% of QWERTY speed with a meaningfully lower error rate and noticeably less fatigue. Dvorak never caught up in the time available.

The error rate result is the interesting one. Colemak-DH keeps common letter pairs on the home row, so fewer keystrokes involve a reach. Fewer reaches means fewer mistakes, and correcting a mistake costs more time than the reach would have.

## Where alternative layouts do win

Three situations where the switch pays for itself regardless of speed:

**Existing strain or RSI symptoms.** Reduced finger travel is a direct reduction in load. If your hands hurt at the end of the day, Colemak-DH's home-row bias is a legitimate intervention, and it worked for me.

**Programmers typing lots of symbols.** Dvorak puts common punctuation under the strongest fingers, which is a genuine ergonomic win for code — but it also moves every shortcut you know, and `Ctrl-C` becomes something your left hand has to relearn.

**People who type under 40 wpm and want to improve.** If you are rebuilding your typing anyway, you are paying the learning cost regardless. You might as well pay it once, on a better layout.

## What I would tell you

If your hands do not hurt and you type above 70 wpm, do not switch. The productivity loss during the transition is real, it is concentrated in the weeks you can least afford it, and the ceiling gain is small.

If your hands do hurt, try Colemak-DH before anything more invasive. Keep QWERTY on a separate OS-level layout so you can switch back for other people's machines, and expect eleven to fourteen days before you stop thinking about it.

The bigger wins, in order: a keyboard with a proper split or tent, a chair that puts your elbows at 90 degrees, and typing lessons that stop you anchoring your wrists. A layout change is the fourth thing on that list, not the first.


## The transition plan that actually works

If you decide to switch, do not do what I did the first time — change the layout on a Monday morning with deadlines. The failure rate for layout switching is almost entirely explained by people attempting it during a busy week.

**Week one: evenings only.** Switch the layout on your personal machine, or on a secondary OS-level profile, and use it for an hour a day outside work. Your typing will be slow and wrong. That is the point.

**Week two: mornings.** Use the new layout for the first two hours of the working day, when you are writing prose and email rather than shipping code. Switch back after lunch. The morning/evening split matters because it gives your hands a daily reset.

**Week three: everything except your editor.** By now the layout is comfortable for prose but your editor muscle memory — shortcuts, symbol keys — is still fighting you. Keep the old layout in the editor only.

**Week four: all in.** Then, and only then, delete the old layout from your input sources. Keeping it available "just in case" guarantees you will fall back to it on the first stressful day and lose a week of progress.

## The cost nobody mentions

For roughly ten days you will be measurably slower, and you will make errors in places where you previously made none — including in passwords, terminal commands and anything where a typo has consequences. Plan for that. Do not switch in the same fortnight as a migration, a launch or a job change.

The upside, if you make it through: reduced finger travel, a lower error rate once the layout settles, and — for me — noticeably less discomfort at the end of a long day. Whether that is worth eleven days of typing badly is a personal calculation, and for most people the honest answer is no.
