## How we measured

Every switch gets a force-travel curve from a calibrated gauge, five samples per model, plus a noise reading at 50 cm with the switch mounted in a standard aluminium plate and struck at a consistent depth.

Two caveats before the numbers. Switch batches vary — the same model from a different production run can differ by 5 g of actuation force. And your keycaps, plate material, case foam and desk surface all change the sound more than the switch does. Treat these as relative comparisons, not absolute truths.

## The force curve matters more than the number

Manufacturers quote **actuation force**, the point where the keypress registers. That number tells you almost nothing about how a switch feels, because feel comes from the shape of the whole curve.

A 45 g linear with a long, even ramp feels lighter than a 45 g linear with a sharp initial resistance. A tactile switch with an early, rounded bump feels softer than one with a late, square bump at the same force. Two switches can share a spec sheet and feel like different products.

The practical version: **bottom-out force** is what your fingers actually experience, because most typists bottom out on every key. A switch rated 45 g actuation but 60 g bottom-out will tire you out over a full working day.

  <span class="callout__label">The ergonomic number</span>
  If you type more than four hours a day, look for a bottom-out force between 45 g and 55 g. Below that, accidental presses climb sharply; above 62 g, finger fatigue shows up in the last hour of the day. Weight is not a proxy for quality.

## Noise is about the housing, not the switch

The loudest part of a keystroke is not the switch — it is the keycap hitting the plate, and the housing bottoming out. A silent-switch variant with a damped stem reduces the switch's contribution, but the cap impact stays.

Our readings at 50 cm, on an aluminium plate with PBT keycaps:

| Switch type | Typical reading | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Silent linear (damped) | 48–54 dB | Quietest; feels slightly mushy to some typists |
| Linear, lubed | 55–61 dB | The common office-safe choice |
| Tactile, unlubed | 60–66 dB | Bump adds a distinct tick |
| Clicky | 68–74 dB | Audible through a closed door |

Mounting the same switch in a plastic plate with foam drops every figure by 3–5 dB. If noise is your constraint, spend on the case before you spend on exotic switches.

## What the numbers say

**For shared offices:** a lubed silent linear at 50 g bottom-out. You will not annoy anyone, and the muted feel takes about three days to stop noticing.

**For typing feel:** a tactile with a rounded early bump in the 50–55 g range. The feedback reduces the force you unconsciously apply, which matters more over long sessions than the raw weight.

**For gaming:** a short-travel linear with a light actuation. Speed matters, and none of the ergonomic considerations above apply when your sessions are an hour long.

The uncomfortable conclusion after testing eleven: most people would be happiest with a mid-weight lubed linear and a decent case, and the difference between that and a boutique switch costing four times as much is smaller than the difference a $20 set of foam inserts makes.


## Lubing: the change that matters more than the switch

Every switch I tested changed character after lubrication, and several changed category. A tactile that felt scratchy and vague became smooth and defined; a linear that felt gritty became the best-feeling switch on the bench.

The process is tedious rather than difficult. Disassemble, apply a thin coat of Krytox 205g0 to the stem rails and the housing rails with a small brush, reassemble. Budget two to three minutes per switch the first time, under a minute once your hands know it. For a 65% board that is an afternoon; for a full-size board it is a weekend you will resent and then defend.

Two warnings. **Do not lube the tactile leaf** on a tactile switch — you will erase the bump. And **use less than you think**: an over-lubed switch feels wet and slow, and the only fix is disassembly and cleaning.

## Mounting and case mods, ranked by effect

If your goal is sound rather than feel, the order of cost-effectiveness is roughly:

1. **Case foam** — $10, cuts hollow resonance by 3–5 dB
2. **Tape mod** on the PCB back — free, adds a lower-pitched "thock"
3. **Lubed stabilisers** — the rattle on a space bar is almost always an unlubed stabiliser, not the switch
4. **Different plate material** — plastic and PC are quieter; aluminium and brass are sharper and louder
5. **Different switches** — the most expensive step with the smallest marginal gain

Notice that switches are last. Most of the sound people attribute to a switch choice is actually the case, the plate and the stabilisers.

## What to buy first

If you are unsure, buy a switch tester rather than a keyboard. Twenty models on one acrylic block costs less than a single set of keycaps, and five minutes with it tells you more than any review — including this one.
