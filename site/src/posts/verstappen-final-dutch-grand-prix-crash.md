## A farewell weekend in the wrong weather

The 2026 Dutch Grand Prix at Zandvoort was never going to be a normal race. It was the final running of the Dutch Grand Prix on the Formula 1 calendar, and it was Max Verstappen's final home race. The orange grandstand was full, the farewell mood was everywhere, and the four-time world champion started from seventh on the grid after battling illness through the weekend.

Then the weather turned. A rain shower hit the circuit roughly forty minutes before lights out. Drivers completed their formation laps to the grid on intermediate tyres, but when the field lined up, all 22 cars took the start on slicks — a collective read that the track would be dry enough by the start. By the end of the first lap, that read had cost Verstappen his race.

## What the rain did to Zandvoort

Zandvoort is not a track where standing water sits still. The wind direction on the day pushed the remaining moisture toward the final sector, making the banked last corner — the S-bend, turn 14, which F1 named after Verstappen himself in 2020 — the wettest part of the circuit. That is an awkward place for water, because the steep banking physically funnels it down the track toward the bottom of the corner, exactly where the racing line runs and where traction is most critical on exit.

Add a damp kerb on the inside and a surface that looked like it was drying in patches, and you have the most dangerous section of the track in the world for a driver on slicks. The track was not uniformly wet and it was not uniformly dry. It was somewhere in between, and "in between" is where misjudgements happen.

## Lap one, corner by corner

The start itself was clean across the field. Verstappen's opening lap was not, though, in the way nobody at home wanted.

Approaching turn one he was squeezed onto the grass by Lewis Hamilton and had to recover from the kerb. From there he settled into a battle for seventh with his Red Bull team-mate, stand-in Liam Lawson, and he came out of the first sector on top of that fight, holding seventh as the lap continued.

Then the car reached the S-bend.

Verstappen dropped a wheel onto the damp inside kerb as he turned in. That is the move that started the crash: a wheel planted on a raised, wet kerb at the base of the banking, at the point of the corner where the water had collected. From there, as he says, he could see what looked like a dry line emerging ahead of him.

## The impact and the red flag

He put the car back on the power. For a moment it appeared to work — the front of the car was rotating out of the corner on grip. Then the rear found the water, traction vanished, and the car spun violently.

The hit with the concrete wall on the outside of the banked corner was heavy. The car spun back across the track into the path of oncoming traffic — there were several cars close behind — and the field threaded past the spinning Red Bull without collecting it. The car then ran to a lighter secondary impact with the inside wall before coming to rest.

The numbers are almost a relief: the impact registered below 15 G, so Verstappen was not required to visit the medical centre, and he walked away from the wreckage under his own power. Over the radio his message was short and apologetic — "Yeah, that's it. Ah, s**t, sorry."

The red flag came out almost immediately. Marshals spent roughly thirty minutes clearing the track, with the rest of the field parked in the pit lane. And the confusion at that corner was not over: just seconds after Verstappen's accident, Audi's Gabriel Bortoleto made a similar error in the same corner, spun without touching the wall, and was perilously close to being collected by his team-mate Nico Hülkenberg, who was extremely fortunate to avoid a T-bone.

## How Verstappen explains it himself

What followed was the part that mattered most to the home crowd: the explanation. Verstappen did not blame the car, the track, or the race control call. He described a classic, if extreme, driver error — reading a drying track and acting on a patch of grip that was not there.

"It was of course still quite wet in that corner," he told Sky Sports F1. "But as soon as I was going through the corner, at one point I could see a little bit of a dry line coming. I thought from this point on I could accelerate again, but suddenly then accelerated a lot and it caught me out. Tried to correct it, but I couldn't."

The rest of the account is what made it sound almost small, which is the understatement of the day: "And then of course, you hit the wall, but it honestly could have been a lot worse because there were a lot of cars behind me as well. But luckily that all went well."

He was asked about F1's wet-weather power limit, which reduces engine output in heavy rain. His answer was that it would not have changed the outcome — the problem was grip and the balance of the car at the moment the rear let go, not power.

## What happened next: a win for Norris, points for Lawson

The race restarted from standing, and it became a championship fight Verstappen was no longer in. Kimi Antonelli showed the way and overtook pole-sitter Lando Norris to lead after the restart, but Norris hunted him down and took the win in 2:04:44.859 — his second consecutive race victory and McLaren's third consecutive win at the Dutch Grand Prix. Antonelli finished second, extending his championship lead to 59 points, with George Russell holding off Lewis Hamilton for third, Charles Leclerc fifth, and Oscar Piastri sixth.

The best day for Red Bull came from the driver Verstappen was supposed to be racing. Lawson, still getting to know the car, finished seventh and took six points — which, in a small but noted wrinkle, count for Red Bull in the constructors' championship rather than for Racing Bulls, the team he nominally drives for.

In the standings, Mercedes leads the constructors' with 425 points, Ferrari 338, McLaren 263 and Red Bull 186. Verstappen, having committed his long-term future to Red Bull, said the team would turn its attention quickly to next season and to Monza — where, in his words, rivals had been out of reach at Zandvoort.

## Why the crash matters beyond the points

Strip away the headlines and the crash is a single driver error in a wet final corner. But it happened at the exact moment when the circuit and the driver were supposed to be the same story.

Zandvoort has been Verstappen's circuit for most of the decade, and the orange wall had been his backdrop since the track returned to the calendar. Instead of a home win to close the final Dutch Grand Prix, the farewell ended with the red flag coming out after a single lap, the grandstand stunned, and a driver who rarely has to hold his hands up saying the only honest thing there was to say: that one was on him.

The bigger story is the gap the race exposed. With the championship running to Mercedes and the Red Bull a clear step behind, the crash will be remembered in two forms at once — as the moment Verstappen's Zandvoort era ended, and as one more marker of a season his team now has to rebuild.
